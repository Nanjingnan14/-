### 12.2 Independent analysis: the eight main lines and the askable interfaces

The interfaces in this section are all readings across slide pages and recordings that can be pointed to by location, not speculation about new facts. Each line gives one question and one answer skeleton, usable directly in an essay.

**1. The state and capital**

The running question of the whole course; W3 asks it twice, "what should be the relationship between the state and capital?". There are three sets of interfaces. L3 slide 43 requires a strong state able to impose discipline on private capital; the SAPs on L4 slide 33 give the opposite answer, with the state withdrawing and prices set by the market. L11 slide 12 pushes the same question to the international level: China's share in the existing institutions is too small, so it builds parallel structures instead. L5 slide 6 calls the SAP a move that "reversed the development project", which the instructor calls a complete U-turn (W5 lines 239–242).

Question: Why did the same state–capital question produce developmental states in East Asia and structural adjustment in Africa?
Skeleton: first give the institutional preconditions of the two answers (a strong state able to discipline capital / creditors able to discipline the state), then one example each (Korea picking winners and protecting them for a limited period / Ghana's 1983 SAP), and finally point out that both are answering "who sets the conditions for capital".

**2. The reproduction of core and periphery**

L2 gives the three-tier structure and surplus extraction, L3 the NIDL division of labour, L9 global cities and care chains, L10 the agricultural division of labour, L11 China's position. L9 slide 3 places Wallerstein's three-tier model and the global city connection diagram on the same page, the most direct juxtaposition in the whole course.

Question: Has the core–periphery structure dissolved, or only changed form?
Skeleton: first acknowledge the change in form (from raw-material extraction to process fragmentation and platform labour), then use one example to show that surplus extraction persists (e-waste chains, logistics outsourcing, agricultural export standards), and finally use the semi-periphery to explain East Asia's rise.

**3. Dispossession and accumulation**

Accumulation by dispossession on L4 slide 29, rural-urban extraction on L9 slide 6 and infrastructural capitalism on slide 13, enclosure on L10 slide 20, the aerial photographs of Liede village and urban village redevelopment on L1 slides 16–21. All four lectures are talking about different stages of the same thing; the slides do not draw them into a single line, and that is precisely where the connection can be built oneself.

Question: Is dispossession a precondition of accumulation, or can accumulation proceed without it?
Skeleton: first give Harvey's proposition, then use the peri-urban land in L9 and Liede in L1 to show how compensation and shares give dispossession the form of exchange, and finally point out that "accumulation without dispossession" still presupposes an asymmetry in land property rights.

**4. Discourse and governance**

The three questions on L1 slide 28 (who defines development, who has the say, who decides who needs development), Foucault's definition of discourse on L5 slide 20, the NPA on L6 slides 6–8 turning poverty into a deliverable project, the debt trap accusation on L11 slide 32, the definition of food security on L10 slide 6. This cluster is where she said the exam would land (Foucault), and also where her own research lands (NGOs from autonomy to governance).

Question: How does the language of development determine what can be done about poverty?
Skeleton: first give the two mechanisms of discourse (constructing the object, limiting what can be said), then use Ferguson's Lesotho to show how technicization strips out the political, and finally use the government purchase of services from NGOs to show how the space for criticism narrows once they are brought into governance.

**5. Gender and structure**

WID→WAD→GAD in L7, GDP counting only market activity on L1 slide 24, care chains and the feminization of survival on L9 slide 9, women agricultural workers on L10 slide 23 (lack of security and rights in land). The instructor flagged this line herself at W11 13:42.

Question: Why does economic growth measured by GDP undercount women's work, and what follows from that?
Skeleton: first give the counting rules of GDP, then use social reproduction and care chains to show that the uncounted part sustains the counted part, and finally land on the policy consequences (CCTs and microfinance transferring responsibility to women).

**6. City and countryside, and land**

"Deliberate shrinking of the agricultural population" on L3 slide 17, rural-urban polarization on L9 slide 6, agricultural modernization and land consolidation in L10, urban villages in L1. Chained together, the four lectures are a history of rural-urban relations in China and the global South.

Question: Is rural decline a by-product of urban growth or a condition of it?
Skeleton: first give the polarization mechanism (a one-way flow of people and resources), then use land finance and land expropriation to show the countryside being drained of people and capitalized at the same time, and finally point out that "rural revitalization" policy re-prices the same land.

**7. The politics of measurement**

GDP / GNP / HDI and country classification in L1, the UN System of National Accounts in L7 recording additions but not costs, gig work statistics in L9 (320 million and 44% in 2026), the 100% self-sufficiency line in L10 and the Singapore counter-example. The instructor said names, theories, institutions and timelines must be known well; measurement sits at the boundary of the institutions category.

Question: What does a development indicator make visible, and what does it hide?
Skeleton: first give the construction of one indicator (GDP counts domestic output, the self-sufficiency rate the ratio of production to consumption), then one counter-example (Singapore, high security and low self-sufficiency), and finally say that the choice of indicator is itself a governance choice.

**8. China's position**

Who defines the three-world labels in L1, the puzzle of East Asia's rise on L2 slides 46 and 51, the developmental state in L3, the dual approach and parallel structures in L11. This line turns "is China a case or an exception" into an answerable question.

Question: Is China's rise a case of the developmental state or a revision of the world-system?
Skeleton: first give the criteria of the developmental state (picking winners, time-limited protection, disciplining capital), then use the dual approach and the AIIB in L11 to show state capacity being used to rewrite the rules, and finally point out that each of the two explanations accounts for half of it.

Of the eight, the first, second, fourth and fifth also connect to the concepts named for the exam (ISI/EOI, food security, Foucault, WID/GAD), so practise them first.
