# APSS5521 Cross-Lecture Connections

> Basis: the 40 verbatim quotations in section 12.1 of the master table of theories and concepts, the slide numbers of each lecture's concept table, and positions in the verbatim transcripts. Only existing connections are analysed; no new facts are added. Slide numbers follow the combined slide transcript.

## 1. Five Ways She Makes Connections

She connects the same thing in five ways; each is recognised by a different marker and used differently in an answer.

| Way | Marker | Typical example | Use in an answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preview | At the end of a lecture she says what comes next week | W1 lines 141–149, four lectures named in sequence | Sequence information only; not an examinable point |
| Handover | A lecture leaves a question unresolved and the next one begins from it | "why many of these programs failed" (W2 segment 2 09:52) | The two lectures share one failure case and can be answered against each other |
| Reversal | The same question is given the opposite answer | ISI in L3 and SAPs in L4 | The substance of comparison questions |
| Stepping back a level | From concrete measures to the level of principle | "we're still thinking about the same issue" (W4 15:00) | The opening sentence of an essay |
| Shift of unit | The same mechanism carried to a different unit of analysis | State in L1, L2 and L3, city in L9 | The boundary of a concept-discrimination question |
| Shared criteria | One evaluative framework applied to different objects | "what works and what does not" (W5 lines 1359–1373) | Shared by presentation questions and essay questions |

She set out the whole structure in the first class: at W1 lines 106–107 she said the course would cover week one to week eleven and then named the weeks one by one. So the connections in each later lecture are not thought up on the spot; they fill in the map drawn in the first class. This explains why her cross-lecture questions are mostly questions about the answer to one question across several lectures, rather than two concepts put side by side.

## 2. The Substance of Each Pairwise Connection

### 1. From the Diagnosis of Dependency to the Prescription of ISI (L2 → L3)

L2 gives the diagnosis: surplus flows from the village through the local, regional and national capital to the West (metropolis–satellite model, L2 slide 36), and underdevelopment is not a natural condition but the result of colonial domination (L2 slide 26). L3 gives the prescription: tariffs, quotas and content regulation to block imports and to nurture the infant industry at home (L3 slides 23–24). The two meet at the comparison table on L3 slide 16, where state-led model is explicitly marked "Draws on dependency theory".

At W4 07:20 she confirmed the lineage, saying that ISI "is a direct sort of child of dependency school", and gave the reason: both come out of Latin America, and both hold that structural obstacles block the Third World, so the state has to push industrialisation hard. This sentence fixes the relation between L2 and L3 as parent and child, not as parallel.

The nature of the connection is a translation from theory into policy. Dependency theory says that surplus is extracted; the response of ISI is to stop trading with them and make things oneself. The theoretical commitment of the two lectures is therefore the same: cut the channel of extraction. This also means that the diagnosis of failure in the second half of L3 has to return to the concepts of L2: once protection turns into licences traded for kickbacks, the state cannot restrain capital (L3 slide 30, Chibber); extraction has not been cut, only the extractor has changed.

Question forms. "Why did dependency theory produce ISI in Latin America and not some other policy": the premise of structural obstacles has to come first, or the answer turns into policy history. "Does the failure of ISI falsify dependency theory": separate two things, the diagnosis was right that extraction exists, the prescription assumed that the state could replace capital, and the latter is where it failed.

### 2. One Failure, Two Diagnoses (L3 → L4)

At the end of L3 the death of ISI is put down to two causes: low efficiency and poor exports (slide 29), corruption and inequality (slide 30). At W3 segment 2 08:36 she said that the next week would be about "this group of economists, neoclassical economists". L4 offers an entirely different diagnosis: the problem is not that the state is too weak but that the state did what it should not do, with deregulation, limited government and market fundamentalism set side by side on slide 29 and the Washington Consensus on slide 30.

Both lectures look at the same 1960s data and give opposite causes. Chibber's version is that the state was unable to restrain private capital; Friedman's version is that state intervention itself was wrong. This connection is the sharpest in the course, because it is the standard structure of one experience and two diagnoses: if an essay asks why ISI failed, the quality of the answer depends on whether the two diagnoses are written separately instead of being merged into a single phrase about government failure.

The policy implications of the two diagnoses differ as well. If the state is too weak, the solution is to build stronger state capacity, the road later taken by the East Asian developmental states (L3 slides 41–43). If the state should not be managing at all, the solution is to shrink the state back, the road taken by the SAPs in L4. One failure is explained in two opposite directions, ten years apart.

Question forms. "Can the failure of ISI prove that neoliberalism is right": point out that neoliberalism uses another reading of the same body of evidence, and that the evidence does not distinguish between the two explanations. "Which diagnosis fits the East Asian experience better": use the three items of picking winners, time-limited protection and restraining capital (L3 slides 42–43) to give an example of a strong state that was also highly efficient.

### 3. From Measures Back to Principles (the Second Connection Between L3 and L4)

After the sentence "last week's lecture are directly connected" at W4 15:00, she asked "what is the relationship between state and capital" four times between 15:10 and 15:37; this is her own definition of the connection. L3 asks the question from industrialisation: how to industrialise, import substitution or export orientation. L4 steps back a level and asks how much space the state and the market should each have.

After that step back, the two models of L3 become two variants of one answer in L4. ISI and EOI both assume that the state has to push industrialisation, and differ only in method and in time limit (the comparison table on L4 slide 5). The genuine third answer appears only in L4: the state should not be doing this at all. So the disagreement inside L3 is a dispute over method, and the disagreement between L3 and L4 is a dispute over principle.

This step is the most useful one for essays. Whenever the question is why development theory turned from state-centred to market-centred, the first sentence should be this stepping back a level: what is at issue is no longer which road to take but whether the state is entitled to build roads at all.

### 4. The Exact Content of the Reversal (L4 → L5)

At W4 segment 1 14:09 she announced that the next week would cover alternative approaches and post-development. L5 slide 6 sets out the relation in one sentence: SAP "reversed" the development project as it prescribed "shrinking the state". At W5 lines 239–241 she called it a complete U-turn.

The specific items of the reversal can be lined up. The premise of the post-war development project is that the state mobilises resources and drives growth through industrialisation (L4 slide 53). The list of SAPs is privatisation of state enterprises, removal of subsidies, removal of minimum wage controls, currency devaluation, removal of tariffs and quotas (L4 slides 33–34). The two lists are almost item-by-item opposites. So reversal is not an adjective; it can be matched item by item.

This connection also carries a consequence, the export-led collapse on L4 slide 50. When seventy countries specialised in exports by comparative advantage at the same time, the result was excess exports and commodity prices falling back to 1930s levels. One of the four criticisms of post-development on L5 slide 50 says that post-development ignores the achievements of development; this is exactly the counter-example to use, since the cost of the reversal can be measured.

Question forms. "Was neoliberalism merely an adjustment of the development project": list the three sets of opposite items and point out that the direction is reversed, not the degree. "Why do SAP prescriptions fail when many countries carry them out at the same time": use the export-led collapse, individual rationality adding up to collective disaster.

### 5. Who Is Entitled to Define Development (L1 → L5)

At W5 lines 498–499 she pointed back to the first class: "I asked you, all these labels, who defines what development is?". L1 slide 30 puts two definitions side by side: development as a spontaneous, universal social process, and development as political intervention. L5 pushes the second to its extreme: development as a whole is a Eurocentric discourse (slide 21), and underdevelopment and the Third World are discursive products of the post-war period (slide 24).

The nature of the connection is the progression of one question. L1 asks who sets the labels; the answer is Europe, America and UN agencies. L5 asks whether the labels hold at all; the answer is that the label is power. What is crossed in between is Foucault's discourse and the power-knowledge nexus (L5 slide 20), which is also the place she named for the exam.

Question forms. "Is development descriptive or normative": begin from the two definitions on L1 slide 30, then use L5 slide 24 to show who produces the description itself. "Why did the concept of the Third World not exist before 1945": use Escobar's phrase "discursive products" (W5 lines 482–487).

### 6. The Place of Lesotho on the Ladder (L5 → L1)

At W5 lines 799–800 she said that Lesotho sits at the first of Rostow's five stages, using the aeroplane metaphor. This sentence pins the L5 case directly onto the ladder of L1 slides 45–48. Lesotho is not an example chosen at random; it is an instance of the position at the bottom of the ladder.

The nature of the connection is the placement of a case. L1 supplies the ladder, L5 supplies a place that is labelled LDC and handled by standardised packages (L5 slides 33, 36). Read together, Rostow's ladder is not an analytical tool but a classifying device that allocates eligibility for intervention.

Question forms. "Are Rostow's five stages and post-development theory completely opposed": point out that the opposition lies in evaluation, not in object, since both are talking about the same places labelled underdeveloped.

### 7. One Prescription Written for Every Country (L5 → L4)

At W5 lines 847–849 she said that we had looked at this chart the week before and that every country received exactly the same prescription. This refers to the SAPs list on L4 slides 33–34 and to the comparative advantage logic on slide 46. L5 uses it as evidence for the post-development criticism of standardised packages (the largely standardized packages on slide 36).

The nature of the connection is that the same material serves opposite functions in two lectures. L4 uses it to describe policy content, L5 uses it to describe the epistemological premise of policy. The former asks what the prescription is, the latter asks why one prescription can be written for seventy different countries.

This is the other side of the same pair of lectures as group 4: group 4 looks at direction, this group looks at uniformity. In essays the two are often used together, first against direction and then against uniformity.

### 8. Criteria Applied to Four Objects (L5 → L6, L7, L10, L11)

W5 lines 1359–1373 set out the design of the seminar questions, asking in succession "What kind of NGOs work next week?", "Programs meant to help women. Where are the gender groups?", "Same for those with food and agriculture", "China and global development". The four questions share one structure: what works, what does not, and why.

This is the only place in the course where she states outright that four lectures share one set of criteria, and it is also the template shared by presentation questions and essay questions. The four objects are an organisation (NGOs), an assisted group (women), a sector (agriculture) and a state (China), and each can be filled into the three cells of works, does not work, and reason.

The nature of the connection is consistency at the methodological level, not a connection of content. She put it more plainly herself at W11 13:35: everything covered in the course is interconnected.

Question forms. "Why can these four topics be asked in the same way": point out that the criterion is effectiveness rather than position, and that all four lectures can supply concrete cases of success and failure.

### 9. The Actor Changes, and So Does the Period (L3, L4 → L6)

W6 05:31–05:42 is a review: what came before was all done by governments, modernisation plans, import substitution, export orientation, various state-led approaches. Then at 05:52 a turn follows, that development is not only done by governments. From 06:12 she moves into the 1980s; at 06:24 she says that if you remember the lecture on neoliberalism you will know that it does not believe in a big state; at 06:40, that it does not believe in the post-war developmental state.

This connection has two layers. The actor changes from the state to NGOs, and the period changes from developmentalism to neoliberalism. The two layers come in order: the retreat of the state in the 1980s comes first, and the shift of donor funding to NGOs comes after (NPA, L6 slide 6).

It also has a reverse echo. The retreat of the state should have reduced governance; what actually happened was a change of hands in governance, with donors steering NGOs from a distance through funding and evaluation (L6 slide 13, resource dependency), and NGOs becoming a "servant of government departments" under government purchase of services (slide 14). The state has not withdrawn; it is simply no longer at the front line.

Question forms. "Is the rise of NGOs the result of state withdrawal or of state restructuring": use two pieces of evidence, the timing of the NPA and government purchase of services, the latter showing that the state is still present.

### 10. Post-Development Used to Judge NGOs (L6 → L5)

At W6 21:47 she said that we had studied Escobar and Ferguson the week before, and then asked "how would post-development theorists look at NGOs?". This sentence turns the theory of L5 directly into the evaluative tool of L6.

Seen through post-development, the question about NGOs is not efficiency but discourse. NGOs turn poverty into a deliverable project, which is precisely Ferguson's anti-politics machine (L5 slide 40). Elite capture in L6 (slide 15) and the withdrawal of the poor from mobilisation (slide 16) are versions of the same judgement at the organisational level.

The nature of the connection is theory used as a ruler. L5 supplies the critical framework, L6 supplies testable objects, and only together do they make a complete argument.

### 11. The Basis of Measurement (L1 → L7)

At W7 15:02 she said that this had been discussed since the first class. L1 slide 24 gives the definitions of GDP and GNP: GDP counts output within the territory regardless of who owns the profit. The UN System of National Accounts on L7 slide 13 records only the additions to GNP, with no debit side: the labour and environmental costs of social reproduction never enter the accounts.

The nature of the connection is two questions asked of one basis of measurement. L1 asks what this indicator measures, L7 asks whom it leaves out. What is left out has a gender structure: unpaid housework, childcare and care work are all uncounted (L7 slide 13), and it is mainly women who do this work.

The most direct examinable form of this connection is a discrimination question of the calculation type. Give the example of a household and ask what GDP records and what it does not; the answer has to point out that the same activity, once performed outside the market, is not counted.

### 12. The Mode of Questioning Comes from Postmodernism (L5 → L7)

W7 18:55–19:19 puts it clearly: what GAD has to ask is how activities are valued, by whom, and for whose benefit, followed by the sentence "you can very clearly see the post-modernist and the post-developments theories impact on the Gad Feminist", the reason being that both are concerned with discourse.

This connection is not a connection of content but of syntax. The definition of discourse on L5 slide 20 governs how a topic can be meaningfully talked about; GAD on L7 slide 13 applies the same mode of questioning to what counts as productive labour. WID asks who is doing what, GAD asks why this activity counts as valuable, and the latter mode of questioning is taken over from discourse analysis.

This is also the second appearance of Foucault in the course. She named Foucault as examinable (W5 line 360), and Foucault actually lands not only on the discourse page of L5 but also in the definition of GAD in L7. The two places have to be read together when revising.

Question forms. "Is the difference between GAD and WID only a difference of focus": look at the difference from the structure of questioning, since WID adds within the existing system of valuation while GAD questions the system of valuation itself.

### 13. The Unit of Analysis Shifts from the State to the City (L1, L2, L3 → L9)

W9 01:33–02:20 is the most important methodological statement in the whole course. She said that dependency theory, modernisation theory and Wallerstein's world-system theory all take the nation-state as the unit of analysis, that China, India, Brazil, Finland and Norway are each one cell. Then the sentence "It was all about the state, the developmental state, the welfare state, the neoliberal state". Then Sassen shifts the unit to the city (L9 slides 2–4).

One detail needs attention here. World-system theory in fact already goes beyond the single state, using the three tiers of core, semi-periphery and periphery (L2 slides 47–51). But she places it alongside dependency and modernisation as state-based, on the ground that each cell in the three tiers is still a state. In the exam this simplification should be written as she puts it; do not correct her on this point.

The shift of unit brings out three new objects: connections between cities forming a new geography of centrality (slide 5, Ny-lon-kong), polarisation inside cities (slide 8), and transnational care chains (slide 9). All three exceed what national statistics can see.

Question forms. "What can be seen with the city as the unit that cannot be seen with the state as the unit": give two examples, financial connections between cities and the stratification of migrant workers within cities. "Does Sassen refute world-system theory": say that the unit differs, the three-tier structure remains, and only the point of application moves from the state to the city.

### 14. A Causal Chain Across Four Lectures (L2, L4, L7 → L9)

W9 13:27–14:53 is the most complete account of a mechanism in the course and is worth taking apart sentence by sentence. She first picks up the group presentation from three weeks earlier (foreign domestic helpers in Hong Kong) and says that it is precisely one kind of polarisation in the global city. Then the care chain: women migrants working for low pay and sending remittances home. Then the key sentence, that these remittances become an important source of hard currency for the governments of the migrants' home countries, on which the Philippines is highly dependent. Then she links to L4, third world economies at the edge of the system, carrying debt and poverty, "from the structural adjustment programs that we talked about before in lecture four", and governments begin to build survival circuits on the backs of women.

Every link in this chain sits in a different lecture. L2 supplies the extraction of surplus at the periphery, L4 supplies debt and SAPs, L7 supplies the gender division of labour and unpaid labour, and L9 joins them into survival circuits and the feminisation of survival (slide 9). Together the four lectures form a writable causal sequence, and every link has a slide and a recording position.

This is also the place where a cross-lecture essay question is most likely. It covers four lectures at once, one named concept (gender) and one mechanism (from debt to care), and it can be brought down to a local case in Hong Kong.

Question forms. "How did structural adjustment change the labour of women in developing countries": write along the chain, not just that women became poorer. "Why is the issue of foreign domestic helpers in Hong Kong part of development studies": enter through the two concepts of the global city and global care chains.

### 15. The Periodisation Lines Up (L3, L4 → L10)

At W10 21:11 she said that the Green Revolution has to be traced back to the first half of the course, the post-war period, the developmental state, Keynesian economics in Europe and in many developing countries, Third World industrialisation. At 24:18 she said that in the fifties and sixties nobody talked about agriculture, everyone talked about industrialisation. At 24:45 the sentence "So this is the age of neoliberalism", followed by the corporate food regime.

These three sentences pin the periodisation of L10 onto the periodisation of L3 and L4. The traditional food system (slide 14) corresponds to the pre-industrial local circuit, the Green Revolution (slide 15) to state direction in the 1950s and 1960s, the corporate food regime (slide 16) to capital-led organisation from the 1980s on. The boundaries of the three periods coincide with the boundaries of L3 and L4.

What the coincidence means is that agriculture is not a separate topic. Each of its turns is driven by a turn in the development regime: the Green Revolution was the state promoting a technological package, the corporate food regime is capital taking over after the state withdrew. The sentence at W10 24:50 about control passing from the state to capital is the agricultural version of shrinking the state on L4 slide 53.

Question forms. "Why did the Green Revolution happen in the 1950s and 1960s": bring in the developmental state and the Keynesian background, not just technological progress. "The relation between the corporate food regime and neoliberalism": use the transfer of control.

### 16. From Dissatisfaction with Institutions to Building Other Institutions (L4 → L11)

W11 23:03–23:41 reviews L4, saying that the dissatisfaction of developing countries was aimed at institutions such as the IMF, and that the anti-IMF riots of the late 1990s and early 2000s arose from the structural adjustment programmes they imposed. Then at 23:41 she turns to the arrival of the AIIB.

L4 slide 40 gives the riot figures: 146 riots in 39 of the roughly 80 debtor countries between 1976 and 1992. L11 slide 12 gives the shares: the United States has a veto in both the IMF and the World Bank and China has none; voting rights in the IMF are 16.49% for the United States against 6.08% for China, and in the World Bank 16% against 5.8%. Only the two sets of numbers joined together make the case for the AIIB: the dissatisfaction comes from the prescriptions, the space comes from the shares.

The nature of the connection is causation at the institutional level. The parallel structures of L11 (slide 10) are not a diplomatic choice that appeared out of nowhere; they are a response to the prescriptions of L4 and to the table of shares in L11.

Question forms. "Why was the AIIB proposed in 2013 and not earlier": use the two backgrounds of stalled quota reform and the anti-IMF riots, and both have to carry numbers.

### 17. Which Lecture a Concept Comes From (the W11 09:22 Exam Technique)

W11 09:22–09:40 is about revision method: she asks twice "which lecture is that from". The first example, import substitution industrialization, points to L3; the second, subcontracting and outsourcing, she calls "the lecture on globalization", which points to L4.

One thing needs attention in the second example. The main definition of outsourcing and subcontracting is on L3 slide 50, the NIDL page, which describes first world firms closing factories at home and contracting production out to third country manufacturers. L4 slides 6 and 47 also discuss it, but there the transnational corporation is the party placing the order. So the main definition of the concept is in one place in L3, and L4 discusses its institutional form in two places. Calling it globalization is not wrong, but if an MC item asks for the definition, the answer points to L3.

This item is not itself a knowledge connection; it is the format she sets for MC items, locating the lecture from the key term. It determines how the keyword list should be arranged for revision: a lecture number after each term is more useful than an explanation.

### 18. Her Two Examples, Two Kinds of Connection (W11 13:42)

W11 13:35–13:54 is the summary of all the connections; her wording was "everything covered in this course is interconnected", followed by two examples, gender connected to neoliberalism, and food and agriculture connected to world-system theory.

The two examples connect in different ways. Gender and neoliberalism are connected through policy: CCTs and microfinance both transfer responsibility to women against the background of neoliberal cuts in public spending (L7 slides 15–16). Food and agriculture and the world system are connected through position: agricultural exporting countries are fixed in the division between core and periphery as suppliers of raw materials and high-value fresh produce (L10 slide 20).

Under the same sentence pattern lie two mechanisms of connection, one through policy instruments and one through structural position. Mixing the two in an essay is the most common place to lose marks, because connection through policy can specify time and causation, while connection through position can only point to a division of labour, and the evidence for the two is different.

## 3. Her Question-Setting Preferences Seen Through the Ways She Connects

Three things recur and can be used directly as rules for answering.

First, when she connects lectures she uses the same question, not the same topic. The relation between state and capital is asked from L3 to L4 (W4 15:00), the right to define development from L1 to L5 (W5 498), the basis of measurement from L1 to L7 (W7 15:02). So the first sentence of a cross-lecture essay should be that shared question, and only then should the answer move lecture by lecture.

Second, when she evaluates a theory she looks at effects, not at position. The four questions at W5 1359–1373 are all about what works and what does not, and the four criticisms of post-development on L5 slide 50 are also criticisms of effects (ignoring the achievements of development, romanticising tradition). Giving a concrete case of success or failure fits her way of writing better than a judgement of position.

Third, her connections often start from the unit. State to city (L9), state to NGO (L6), production to reproduction (L7). Each of these shifts brings out new objects, and each can become the entry point of an essay question.

## 4. Connection Density and Order of Priority

Counting the 40 verbatim quotations in 12.1, the most connected lecture is L4 (11 times), then L3 (10 times), L2 and L1 (6 each), L5 (5), and the remaining five lectures 1 to 4 times each. This ranking does not coincide with the quiz hotspots: a heavily connected lecture is a hub, which does not make it an examinable point.

The table below lists only the twelve groups that need priority practice; the other six are in the text above.

## 5. Three Points on Stated Locations

W11 23:31 says that SAPs are in "lecture five and lecture six"; in this course they are actually on L4 slides 33–34, so they are recorded as L4. L5 and L6 each have one page reviewing SAPs (L5 slide 6, L6 slide 6), and these two review pages may be what she meant.

"the lecture on globalization" at W11 09:40 refers to L4, but the full definition of subcontracting and outsourcing is on L3 slide 50; both lectures have it, and the main definition is in L3.

"lecture four" at W9 14:37 refers to L4, consistent with the table in 12.1. Another phrase in the same lecture, "back in last year", still carries `to be verified` as to which lecture it refers to.
