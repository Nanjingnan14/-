### 第 24 张｜MEASUREMENTS OF DEVELOPMENT

MEASUREMENTS OF DEVELOPMENT
▪Gross Domestic Product (GDP)
Value of all goods and services produced within a country, regardless of whether the profits are claimed by national or foreign individuals/firms
▪Gross National Product (GNP) or Gross National Income (GNI)
Value of all goods and services claimed by residents of a country, regardless of where production took place (includes income overseas)
▪Development viewed largely in economic terms
▪The World Bank uses GNI per capita to categorize countries: low-income/ lower-middle-income/ upper-middle-income/ high-income.
▪Assumption that greater economic wealth equates better livelihoods, health, education, etc.
Willis (2005: 3-4)

### 第 35 张｜THE DEVELOPMENT PROJECT AFTER WW2

THE DEVELOPMENT PROJECT AFTER WW2
▪Decolonization:
Former colonies gaining independence as sovereign “nation states”
1945-1981: 105 new states joined the United Nations
Age of optimism: Governments and peoples coming together to stimulate economic growth; improve education, healthcare, housing, transportation;
promote democracy and citizenship in the newly formed nation states.
“Development” as an emancipatory promise: based on “the ideal of self-governing states composed of citizens united by the ideology of nationalism” vs. colonial rule over subjects (“civilizing inferior races”)
McMichael (2005: 4, 13)

### 第 47 张｜1. Traditional society

1. Traditional society
▪Agricultural society
▪Pre-Newtonian science and technology
▪Family and clan relationships; “pre-nation state”
2. Preconditions for take-off
▪National level organizations and institutions
▪Rise of individualistic values: rational, risk taking, able to defer pleasure and immediate consumption
▪Savings and investment rates above population growth rates

### 第 48 张｜3. Take-off

3. Take-off
▪Stimulus to take-off needed:
➢Political revolution and restructuring of major institutions
➢Technological innovation
➢Favorable international environment with rising export demands and prices
▪Critical factor: rate of productive investments and savings must reach 10% of national income (otherwise economic growth cannot overtake rate of population growth)

### 第 49 张｜4. Drive to maturity

4. Drive to maturity
▪Once growth has become an automatic process, the fourth stage is reached (“cruising altitude”)
▪Extended range of technology adopted; development of new sectors
5. Age of high mass consumption
▪Growth in employment opportunities
▪Increase in national income
▪Formation of strong domestic market; widespread consumption of consumer goods and services
▪Increase in welfare spending

### 第 55 张｜CRITIQUING MODERNIZATION APPROACH

CRITIQUING MODERNIZATION APPROACH
▪Modernization is essentially understood as Westernization
▪Highly general, does not pay attention to the range of societies in the
South, their histories, cultures, and needs
▪James Scott’s critique of “high modernism”:
▪Overconfidence in science and technical planning in redesigning society
▪Central plans ignore local complexities
▪Technical knowledge (techne) vs Practical local knowledge (mētis): The first being formal, universal, scientific; the latter experiential and context-specific

### 第 56 张｜CRITIQUING MODERNIZATION APPROACH

CRITIQUING MODERNIZATION APPROACH
▪Focus on internal sources of change within the nation-state: Development seen as a domestic matter.
▪Krishna (2009): Rostow basically treats different nations as runners positioned in distinct lanes in a race track
▪Each country runs in its own lane
▪Some countries start the race earlier (Britain →
Germany and US …) while others are just entering the race or heading to the stadium
▪The track condition is the same for all
→ Countries have themselves to blame for their success or failure in taking off

### 第 26 张｜Frank’s answer: It is NOT a coincidence

Frank’s answer: It is NOT a coincidence
▪Development (of the west) and underdevelopment (of the rest) are two sides of the same process, resulting in polarization
▪Many “traditional” countries were quite advanced before they encountered colonialism in the 18th century, e.g. India and China
▪The reason for their underdevelopment is external: Colonialism and foreign domination have reversed development in Third World countries, and forced them on a path of economic backwardness
▪Underdevelopment is not natural: it is the result of a long history of colonial domination

COLONIALISM
▪The age of modern colonialism by Western powers began in the
1400s-1500s
▪1488: Discovery of sea route around the southern coast of Africa
▪1492: Discovery of America
▪Main European colonial powers: Spain, Portugal, Holland (Dutch
Republic), France, England.
▪Later also Belgium, Germany and Italy during the period of New
Imperialism (1881-1914) and the “Scramble for Africa”.

### 第 49 张｜Each region plays a functionally specific role in the worldw

Each region plays a functionally specific role in the worldwide division of labour:

### 第 50 张｜▪Unbalanced economic structure between core and periphery

▪Unbalanced economic structure between core and periphery
▪Core countries exploit periphery countries in terms of human and natural resources
▪Periphery countries are financially and technologically dependent on core countries
▪This imbalance limits development in periphery countries, resulting in “underdevelopment” and “dependent development”
▪The semi-periphery acts as a buffer between core and periphery
▪Exploited by core, but also exploits periphery
▪Partially deflects political pressure and prevents periphery countries from forming a united front against the core
▪Helps stabilize the global capitalist system by acting as a political buffer zone

▪Avoids the deterministic statement of dependency theory, i.e.
periphery is bound to suffer underdevelopment because core countries always exploit it
▪Can study why some countries (e.g. East Asia) are able to rise above their peripheral status

### 第 24 张｜LOGIC OF ISI

LOGIC OF ISI
▪Instead of importing First World goods, Third World countries to produce those goods themselves
▪Strategy: Restrict foreign imports by measures such as (1) tariffs (2) quotas (3) content regulations
▪These restrictions raised the price of foreign imports
▪Local industries (who normally could not compete with foreign suppliers) now faced a benign market and could begin producing the good locally
▪Governments also supported these infant industries through:
▪Licensing schemes: limit the number of local firms allowed to produce a given product, in effect giving them a legal monopoly
▪Provision of subsidies and cheap credit
(Rapley 2007: 30-33)

### 第 25 张｜POPULARITY OF ISI

POPULARITY OF ISI
▪Turkey and Latin America as earliest adopters in the 1930s
▪Chile as the boldest experimenter: 140 public firms created by the government between 1940 and 1970
▪Import licensing and tariffs (sometimes exceeding 100%) sheltered the local market from foreign competition
▪After WW2, spread to Middle East and South Asia
▪India adopted Soviet-style planning with a focus on heavy industry, together with a tightly-controlled private economy where local firms were favored by protective tariffs and import prohibition
▪As African countries gained independence in the 1960s, many also adopted ISI
(Rapley 2007: 35-41)

### 第 41 张｜DEVELOPMENTAL STATE

DEVELOPMENTAL STATE
▪Neoclassical economists: Asian NIEs adopted market-based policies and participated in the world market →success

▪Set controls on prices and capital outflows

DIFFERENCES FROM ISI

### 第 11 张｜APPROACHES TO GENDER AND DEVELOPMENT

APPROACHES TO GENDER AND DEVELOPMENT
▪The influence of feminism on development began with the first UN
World Conference on Women, held in Mexico City in 1975
▪1950s and 1960s:
▪Welfare measures mainly targeted women in their domestic role
▪Addressing practical gender needs, e.g. food aid, health, nutrition
▪Women largely viewed as passive
▪1970s:
▪UN declared 1976-1985 the “UN Decade for Women”
▪Women in Development (WID) movement
McMichael (2012)

### 第 12 张｜WOMEN IN DEVELOPMENT (WID)

WOMEN IN DEVELOPMENT (WID)
▪Agenda of equality:
▪Women’s contributions to development have not been recognized: Role as food producers, carers, educators vs. (male) cash-earning activities
▪Problem identified by WID feminists: “women’s inequality of access to and participation in the definition of economic structures and policies and the productive process itself”
▪Integrationist approach:
▪Women often perform unpaid household/farm labor → incorporate them more into income-earning activities
▪Removing obstacles to women’s advancement in the public sphere, e.g.
Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women
(CEDAW), adopted by UN assembly in 1979
▪Focus on legislative changes, e.g. women’s right to own land
McMichael (2012)

### 第 13 张｜GENDER AND DEVELOPMENT (GAD)

GENDER AND DEVELOPMENT (GAD)
▪GAD feminism argues that remedies need to go even further:
▪Not enough to address who is doing what and whether it is valued → ask
HOW activities come to be valued, by whom, and in whose interests
▪Critique of development discourse:
▪What counts as “productive” work under our economic model?
▪Value is attached to a narrow range of cash-generating activities
▪UN System of National Accounts records only additions to GNP, but not
“costs”: the invisible work of social reproduction, costs to the environment, etc. No debit side.
▪“Agenda-setting” approach:
▪Directly challenge conventional development thinking with a feminist perspective (Jahan 1995)
McMichael (2012)

### 第 14 张｜FROM EQUALITY TO EMPOWERMENT

FROM EQUALITY TO EMPOWERMENT
▪Declaration for Economic Justice and Women’s Empowerment (1999):
▪Redefine economic efficiency to include and value women’s unpaid and paid work
▪Efficiency should be measured by effective realization of human development and human rights, rather than growth, trade, corporate profits
▪Changing gender relations through “empowerment”
▪Rise of transnational feminist networks and movements, e.g. anti-sweatshop movement (Kabeer 2018)
▪Projects designed and run by women from the South
▪Female-only policy interventions
Moser (1993), Willis (2011),
McMichael (2012)

### 第 15 张｜THEORY → PRACTICE (?)

THEORY → PRACTICE (?)
Conditional cash transfer
▪To “empower” women by giving them cash, in exchange for “co-responsibility”:
▪Ensuring their children’s attendance at school and medical check-ups
▪Doing “voluntary” community work, e.g. cleaning schools and health centers
Microfinance
▪To “empower” women by giving them micro-loans: access to credit
Intended achievements
▪Greater female control over household income
▪More direct engagement with public institutions: build relationships between women and the state and public policy
[图]

### 第 16 张｜THEORY → PRACTICE (?)

THEORY → PRACTICE (?)
▪These schemes are supposed to support and empower women, but had complex actual effects (Chant 2015)
▪Intensifying their paid and unpaid work
▪Conditional cash transfer ended up placing even more pressure on women and intensified their unpaid maternal and community role → women end up as “duty-bearers for household poverty alleviation” (Chant 2016)
▪For microfinance, pressure of loan repayment forced women to work even harder as wage laborers at the expense of building up their own enterprises
▪Reinforcing traditional gender norms
▪Women and girls as “chief victims and caretakers” (Resurrection 2011): No attempt to involve men and boys in the process
▪“Feminization of responsibility and/or obligation”: Entrenchment of non-egalitarian model of the family
▪“Women end up working for development, rather than development serving primarily to further women’s interests” (Chant 2016)
