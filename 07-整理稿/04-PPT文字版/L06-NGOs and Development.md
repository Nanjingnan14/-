## 第 6 张｜THE RISE AND RISE OF NGOS

THE RISE AND RISE OF NGOS
1980s: Change in approach to development
▪End of Cold War and rise of neoliberal economics
▪The role of the state as the key player in development has come under challenge
▪Move away from top-down approaches; increased support for non-governmental organizations (NGOs)
▪“New Policy Agenda” (NPA) promoted by bilateral and multilateral donor agencies: funding to NGOs and grassroots organizations to alleviate poverty, provide welfare, develop civil society

## 第 7 张｜Economic logic of NPA:

Economic logic of NPA:
▪Markets and private sector initiatives are seen as the most efficient mechanisms
▪NGOs viewed to be more efficient and cost-effective service providers than governments, giving better value-for-money
▪Governments in developing countries regarded as “bloated”,
“inefficient” and “corrupt”: NGOs as a potential corrective and preferred channel for social welfare
Edwards and Hulme (1996); Smith (2010)

## 第 8 张｜Political logic of NPA:

Political logic of NPA:
▪“Good governance” considered to be essential for healthy economy
▪Promotion of “civil society” as a key institutional engine in processes of democratization and development
▪NGOs are valued as an integral component of a robust civil society and an important counterweight to state power (“the missing middle” between citizens and the state):
▪Channels of communication and participation
▪Training ground for activists; empowerment
▪Promotion of pluralism
Edwards and Hulme (1996); Smith (2010)

## 第 9 张｜▪Increasing amount of money going through and to NGOs:

▪Increasing amount of money going through and to NGOs:
▪More bilateral aid is channeled through NGOs
▪NGOs are receiving more official aid and becoming more dependent on it
▪Government grants make up 50-90% of budgets of major NGOs in Europe, most of which is eventually passed on to NGOs in developing countries
▪“Explosive” growth of NGOs as a result of funding increase:
▪Nepal: Number of registered NGOs grew from 220 in 1990 to 1,210 in
1993 during a donor “spending spree”
▪Tunisia: 1,886 NGOs in 1988 →5,186 in 1991
▪More Southern NGOs were getting direct funding from bilateral/multilateral donors (rather than through Northern NGOs)
Edwards and Hulme (1996)

## 第 11 张｜DO NGOS DO WHAT THEY ARE SUPPOSED TO?

DO NGOS DO WHAT THEY ARE SUPPOSED TO?
In theory
▪Autonomous, independent from external interests
▪Willingness to confront those in power
▪Closeness to poor people
▪Facilitate democratization
▪Long time horizons for capacity-building
In reality (?)
▪Dependent on external funding
▪Need to answer to donor’s preferences and goals
▪Quantifiable results; short-term projects; output-oriented
▪Entrench corruption

## 第 12 张｜Cost effectiveness vs social effectiveness

Cost effectiveness vs social effectiveness
▪Cost-effective service provision usually rests on standardized delivery systems and internal structures able to manage large amounts of external funding
▪Donors unable or unwilling to support slow careful work with gradual, often non-quantifiable results
▪Aid for infrastructure and long-term projects has dropped from 53% to 31% in ten years’ time from 1990s to 2000s
▪On the one hand want institutional development and capacity building, on the other hand employ short-term, output-oriented methodologies: “quick wins”, immediate results
Glennie (2008); Edwards and Hulme (1996)

## 第 13 张｜Resource dependency

Resource dependency
▪Financial dependence on state actors reduces autonomy
▪External funding: Suspicion of foreign influence, not trusted to take part in domestic policies
▪Local government funding: Less willing to speak out against government;
“creeping compromise”
▪Accountability to donors above vs service to the grassroots
▪Appraisal, monitoring and evaluation criteria: do they really match local needs?
▪“Accountancy” instead of “accountability”
Edwards and Hulme (1996)

## 第 14 张｜Relationship with government

Relationship with government
▪Government units purchasing services from NGOs on a contract basis
(zhengfu goumai fuwu) →NGOs treated as “servant of government departments” and “foot soldiers”, given heavy administrative duties by local officials (Chan and Lei 2017)
▪Competition for government contracts undermines collaboration between NGOs
▪Large and successful NGOs “crowding out” smaller sister agencies, dominating both resources and ideas →contrary to pluralism
▪Pressure to win contracts undermines transparency: NGOs under pressure to perform “public relations activity” (reporting good results but hiding the bad)

## 第 15 张｜Corruption

Corruption
▪In some countries, external funding for NGOs ended up in the pockets of the rich and powerful
▪Smith’s study of NGOs in Nigeria (2010):
▪NGOs were “invented” by elites and politicians and used as a façade
▪Funding used to organize activities and grand events attended by the elites
▪A disproportionate share of development aid was funneled into their own pockets and overseas bank accounts
▪NGOs helped politicians create a positive image of “doing good”
▪Popular cynicism amongst ordinary people toward development programs

## 第 16 张｜THEN... DO WE WANT/NEED NGOS?

THEN... DO WE WANT/NEED NGOS?
A provocative view from Petras and Veltmeyer (2001):
▪NGOs focus on projects: goals are short term and programs are often designed based on neoliberal approaches
▪Funding and evaluation are dependent on “imperial centers and their institutions”
▪Emphasize “self-help” →Demobilize the poor
▪Leaders of movements and trade unions co-opted by NGOs: higher pay, reputation, office staff, relative security from repression →Further weaken movements
